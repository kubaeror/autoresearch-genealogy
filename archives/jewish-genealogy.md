# Jewish Genealogy in Poland

## Introduction

Poland was the heart of Ashkenazi Jewish civilization for over 800 years. By 1939, approximately 3.3 million Jews lived within Polish borders, representing the largest Jewish community in Europe and roughly 10% of the country's population. Cities like Warsaw, Łódź, Kraków, and Lwów housed major Jewish populations, while thousands of smaller towns (shtetlach) were predominantly Jewish.

Polish Jewish genealogy presents distinct challenges. Records span three partition powers (Russia, Austria, Prussia), each with different administrative systems, languages, and record-keeping practices. The Holocaust destroyed approximately 90% of Polish Jewry, along with countless community records, synagogues, and cemeteries. Families were scattered across continents, and survivors often had no documentation of their origins.

Despite these losses, a remarkable quantity of records survived. Polish state archives preserved civil registration books. Soviet authorities maintained metric books as government property. Memorial books (Yizkor bikher) documented destroyed communities. Dedicated volunteers have spent decades indexing surviving records, making Polish Jewish genealogy more accessible than ever.

## Key Polish Jewish Genealogy Resources

### JRI-Poland (Jewish Records Indexing - Poland)

- **URL**: https://jri-poland.org/
- **Coverage**: The primary index for Jewish vital records in Polish archives. Contains millions of indexed birth, marriage, and death records from the 19th and 20th centuries.
- **Town coverage**: Searchable by town. Check the "Town Search" feature to see which records are indexed for a specific locality.
- **How to use**: Search by surname and town. Results show year, record type, and archive location. The index provides Act Number (numer aktu) needed to request images.
- **Requesting images**: JRI-Poland provides archive contact information and sample request letters. Many records can be ordered directly from Polish state archives (Archiwum Państwowe) for a small fee.

### Geneteka

- **URL**: https://geneteka.genealodzy.pl/
- **Coverage**: Free index to Polish vital records, including Jewish metrical records (akta metrykalne wyznania mojżeszowego). Strong coverage for Congress Poland (Królestwo Polskie).
- **Searching for Jewish records**: Select "Wyznanie" (Religion) filter and choose "mojżeszowe" or "żydowskie" to limit results to Jewish records.
- **Naming patterns**: Jewish records often show both the civil name and Hebrew name. A birth record might list "Mojżesz syn Abrahama" (Moses son of Abraham). Mothers' names are typically included, which is valuable for tracing maternal lines.
- **Cross-referencing**: Geneteka links to Metryki.genealodzy.pl for record images when available.

### Szukaj w Archiwach

- **URL**: https://szukajwarchiwach.gov.pl/
- **Coverage**: The Polish state archives' official search portal. Contains digitized record images and finding aids.
- **Jewish community records**: Search for "akta gmin wyznaniowych żydowskich" (Jewish community records) or "akta gminy żydowskiej" plus town name.
- **Rabbinical court records**: Search for "sąd rabinacki" for rabbinical court documentation including marriage contracts, divorces, and conversions.
- **Business and property records**: Search for "księgi hipoteczne" (mortgage books) and "akta notarialne" (notarial records) which often document Jewish merchants and property owners.
- **Navigation**: Results are in Polish. Use browser translation. Image viewers require Java or specific browser settings.

### Gesher Galicia

- **URL**: https://www.geshergalicia.org/
- **Coverage**: Records from the former Austrian province of Galicia (now divided between Poland and Ukraine). Includes southeastern Poland: Kraków, Tarnów, Rzeszów, Przemyśl, and surrounding regions.
- **All-Galicia Database**: Searchable index combining vital records, tax records, census data, and other sources. Search by surname or town.
- **Cadastral maps**: Historical property maps showing Jewish-owned buildings, synagogues, and cemeteries. Essential for understanding shtetl geography.
- **Town research groups**: Active projects for specific Galician towns with dedicated researchers.
- **Membership**: Free basic access; full database access requires membership.

### YIVO Archives

- **URL**: https://www.yivo.org/
- **Coverage**: The YIVO Institute for Jewish Research in New York holds extensive collections related to Polish Jewry.
- **Key holdings**: Landsmanshaftn (hometown society) records, community histories, personal papers, photographs, and the Territorial Collection organized by Polish town.
- **Holocaust-era records**: Documentation from ghettos, underground organizations, and survivor testimonies.
- **Yizkor books**: Major collection of memorial books documenting destroyed communities. Many are partially translated on JewishGen.

### Yad Vashem

- **URL**: https://yvng.yadvashem.org/
- **Central Database of Shoah Victims' Names**: Over 4.8 million names, heavily weighted toward Polish victims.
- **Pages of Testimony**: One-page memorial forms submitted by survivors and relatives. Include name, birth date, town, family relationships, and fate. Often the only surviving documentation for an individual.
- **Community records**: Documentation collected from Jewish communities before destruction.
- **How to search**: Search by name, place of birth, place of death, or family relationships. Polish town names may appear in Yiddish, Polish, German, or Russian spellings.

## Record Types

### Vital Records (Akta Stanu Cywilnego) 1808-1942

Jewish vital records in Poland follow the same civil registration format as Catholic records but were maintained in separate books (księgi wyznania mojżeszowego).

- **Akta urodzenia (Birth records)**: Include date, place, parents' names and ages, father's occupation, witnesses. Hebrew name often recorded alongside civil name.
- **Akta małżeństw (Marriage records)**: Include names and ages of bride and groom, parents' names, occupations, witnesses. Often most detailed records, listing grandparents or prior marriages.
- **Akta zgonów (Death records)**: Include name, age, occupation, parents' names, cause of death, informant.

Records were kept by civil registrars, not rabbis. Language depends on partition and period: Polish (1808-1868 in Congress Poland), Russian (1868-1915 in Congress Poland), German (Prussian partition), Polish (after 1918).

### Rabbinical Records

Separate from civil registration, rabbinical authorities maintained religious documentation:

- **Ketubbot (כתובות)**: Marriage contracts written in Aramaic, specifying bride's dowry and husband's obligations. Often contain Hebrew names and father's names.
- **Get (גט)**: Religious divorce documents. Required for remarriage under Jewish law.
- **Conversion records (גיור)**: Documentation of converts to Judaism.
- **Communal registries**: Some kehillot maintained birth and death registers predating civil registration.

Most rabbinical records were destroyed in the Holocaust. Survivors occasionally brought documents to new countries.

### Census and Tax Records

Pre-1808 research depends heavily on census and tax documentation:

**Russian Partition (Congress Poland)**:
- **Rewizje (Revision Lists)**: Russian Empire censuses from 1795 to 1858. List all household members by name, age, relationship, and occupation. Essential for pre-civil-registration research. Held in Russian and Polish archives.

**Austrian Partition (Galicia)**:
- **Konskrypcje (Conscription Lists)**: Military census records listing adult males. Include age, occupation, physical description.
- **Josephinische Landesaufnahme**: Late 18th-century census and tax records.
- **Franziszeische Kataster**: 1820s-1840s cadastral surveys with property owner names.

**Prussian Partition**:
- **Judenregister**: Jewish registers maintained by Prussian authorities before civil registration (1874).
- **Seelenlisten**: "Soul lists" enumerating Jewish community members.

### Community Records

- **Pinkas kehilla (פנקס קהילה)**: Community registers maintained by the kehilla (organized Jewish community). Include membership, tax assessments, charitable distributions, and community decisions. Most were destroyed; survivors are held in archives worldwide.
- **Chevra kadisha (חברא קדישא) records**: Burial society documentation including death dates, burial locations, and sometimes biographical notes.
- **Synagogue records**: Membership lists, seat assignments (valuable for identifying wealth and status), and special events.

## Jewish Naming in Poland

### Surname Adoption

Fixed surnames were mandated at different times in each partition:

- **Austrian Partition (Galicia)**: 1787 under Joseph II. Earliest mandatory adoption. Some families had surnames earlier.
- **Prussian Partition**: 1797. Similar to Austrian requirements.
- **Russian Partition (Congress Poland)**: Timing varied by region. Generally 1821-1844, though enforcement was inconsistent. Some families used patronymics well into the 1850s.

### Common Surname Types

- **Occupational**: Krawiec (tailor), Złotnik (goldsmith), Szklarz (glazier), Szewc (shoemaker), Kowal (blacksmith)
- **Geographic**: Warszawski (from Warsaw), Krakowski (from Kraków), Lubliner (from Lublin), Poznański (from Poznań)
- **Descriptive**: Klein (small), Gross (large), Schwartz/Szwarc (black), Weiss (white)
- **Patronymic**: Abramowicz (son of Abraham), Izraelowicz (son of Israel), Moszkowicz (son of Moshe)
- **Artificial/Assigned**: Some surnames were assigned by officials, sometimes for fees. Pleasant names like Goldberg, Rosenthal, or Lilienthal sometimes required payment.

### Given Names

Every Jewish person had two names:

- **Hebrew name (shem hakodesh)**: Used for religious purposes, called to Torah, marriage documents, tombstones. Format: "Moshe ben Avraham" (Moses son of Abraham) or "Rivka bat Yitzchak" (Rebecca daughter of Isaac).
- **Civil/secular name**: Used in government records. May be a translation, transliteration, or unrelated name. "Moshe" might appear as Mojżesz (Polish), Moisei (Russian), or Moritz (German).

Multiple transliterations are common. The same name might appear as:
- Chaim / Chaym / Haim / Hajm / Hyam
- Berel / Ber / Dov / Bernard
- Sura / Sarah / Sora / Zara

### Matronymics

Earlier records sometimes use matronymics, especially when the father was deceased or unknown:
- "Mojżesz syn Sary" (Moses son of Sarah)
- "Icek syn Rywki" (Isaac son of Rebecca)

This is valuable for research but requires attention: the same person might appear under father's name in one record and mother's name in another.

## Research Strategies

### Starting Points

1. **Gather family stories**: Record every name, date, and place, especially the town name (even approximate spellings). Interview older relatives immediately.
2. **Search JRI-Poland by town**: Identify which records are indexed for your ancestral town. Note archive locations and Act Numbers.
3. **Check Geneteka**: Search for indexed records with images. Filter by Jewish religion.
4. **Search Szukaj w Archiwach**: Look for digitized images and finding aids.
5. **Consult Yizkor books**: Search JewishGen's Yizkor Book database and read the relevant memorial book. These contain names, photographs, and community history.
6. **Order records from archives**: Use Act Numbers from indexes to request images from Polish state archives.

### Holocaust Research

- **Start with Yad Vashem**: Search the Central Database. Check both victim searches and the Pages of Testimony database.
- **ITS/Arolsen Archives**: Search for camp records, displaced persons documentation, and tracing files at https://arolsen-archives.org/
- **Pages of Testimony**: These may contain family information submitted by relatives. A Page for one family member might name parents, siblings, spouse, and children.
- **Ghetto records**: Some ghetto administrations maintained detailed registries. Warsaw Ghetto records are partially preserved. Łódź Ghetto records are extensive.

### Language Challenges

Polish Jewish records appear in multiple languages:
- **Polish**: 1808-1868 (Congress Poland), post-1918 everywhere
- **Russian**: 1868-1915 (Congress Poland)
- **German**: Prussian partition records, some Austrian records
- **Hebrew/Yiddish**: Rabbinical documents, tombstones, Yizkor books

The same person's name may be transliterated differently in each language. Build a list of all possible spelling variants.

## Regional Variations

### Congress Poland (Królestwo Polskie / Russian Partition)

- Civil registration began in 1808 under Napoleonic influence. Among the earliest civil registration in Eastern Europe.
- Records in Polish until 1868, then Russian (Cyrillic script) until 1915, then Polish again.
- JRI-Poland and Geneteka have strong coverage.
- Archives: Regional state archives (Archiwum Państwowe) throughout central Poland.

### Galicia (Austrian Partition)

- Earlier surname adoption (1787).
- Administrative language German; records often in German or Latin.
- Gesher Galicia is the best starting point.
- Records split between Poland (western Galicia) and Ukraine (eastern Galicia).
- Archives: Archiwum Główne Akt Dawnych (Warsaw), regional archives in Kraków, Przemyśl, Rzeszów. Ukrainian records in Lviv (CDIAL).

### Prussian Partition (Wielkopolska, Pomerania, Silesia)

- German records. Civil registration from 1874.
- Many records destroyed in WWII.
- Pre-1874 records in church books and Jewish community registers (Judenregister).
- Archives: Regional state archives, some records in Berlin (Geheimes Staatsarchiv).

## Shtetl Finder and Town Identification

Identifying the correct ancestral town is the most critical step.

**Common problems**:
- Many towns share similar names: Brzeziny (multiple), Białystok vs. Biała, Kalisz vs. Kalish vs. Kalusz
- Names changed under different administrations: Łódź = Lodz = Lodzh; Lwów = Lviv = Lemberg
- Yiddish names differ from Polish: Lublin = לובלין (Lublin), Warsaw = ווארשע (Varshe)

**Tools**:
- **JewishGen Communities Database**: Match phonetic name variants to specific towns
- **ShtetlSeeker**: Phonetic search for town names
- **Gesher Galicia Gazetteer**: For Galician towns
- **Where Once We Walked**: Published gazetteer of Central and Eastern European Jewish communities

## Holocaust Records

### Sources

- **ITS/Arolsen Archives**: Concentration camp records, DP camp registrations, tracing service files. Free online access.
- **Transporty deportacyjne (Deportation lists)**: Transport manifests listing individuals deported to camps. German records, often with Polish town origins noted.
- **Ghetto records**: Warsaw Ghetto archive (Ringelblum Archive) partially survived. Łódź Ghetto maintained detailed registries of inhabitants.
- **Camp records**: Intake forms, labor assignments, death records. Coverage varies by camp.

### Post-War Records

- **DP camp registrations**: Detailed forms including pre-war address, family members, and intended destination.
- **UNRRA and IRO files**: Refugee assistance organization records.
- **Emigration records**: Ship manifests from European ports to Israel, Americas, Australia.

## Cemeteries

Most Jewish cemeteries in Poland were destroyed during the Holocaust. Tombstones were used as paving stones, building materials, or simply shattered. However:

- **Surviving cemeteries**: Some cemeteries in larger cities survived or have been partially restored (Kraków, Łódź, Warsaw Okopowa).
- **Documentation projects**: Volunteers have photographed and transcribed surviving tombstones.
- **Virtual Shtetl**: https://sztetl.org.pl/ documents Jewish heritage sites including cemeteries and their current condition.
- **JOWBR (JewishGen Online Worldwide Burial Registry)**: Search for indexed cemetery inscriptions.
- **Lapidaria**: Some communities created memorial walls using recovered tombstone fragments.

Tombstones (matzevot) provide Hebrew names, parents' names, death dates, and sometimes biographical information that exists nowhere else.

## Search Tools

### Daitch-Mokotoff Soundex

Standard Soundex was designed for English names and fails on Eastern European Jewish names. The Daitch-Mokotoff Soundex system handles:
- Multiple pronunciations (Ashkenazi vs. Galitzianer Hebrew)
- Slavic consonant clusters
- Yiddish diminutives

JewishGen uses Daitch-Mokotoff by default. Always search using this system rather than exact spelling.

### JewishGen Databases

- **Family Tree of the Jewish People**: User-submitted family trees
- **Communities Database**: Town information and jurisdictional history
- **All-Poland Database**: Combined index of JRI-Poland records
- **Yizkor Book necrology database**: Extracted names from memorial books

## Common Pitfalls

- **Assuming records were destroyed**: Many Polish Jewish records survived in state archives. Check JRI-Poland and Szukaj w Archiwach before concluding records are lost.
- **Searching only one spelling**: Names appear in Polish, Russian, German, and Yiddish variants. Search all possibilities.
- **Ignoring women's maiden names**: Polish records consistently record mothers' maiden names. This is valuable for tracing maternal lines.
- **Stopping at the Holocaust**: Pre-war records extend back to 1808, and census/tax records go earlier. Trace the family backward from known Holocaust-era documentation.
- **Overlooking Yizkor books**: Even if your specific family is not named, Yizkor books describe community structure, key families, and historical context essential for understanding your ancestors' world.
