# The Three Partitions of Poland (1795-1918)

A genealogical research guide covering record types, languages, and archives for each partition territory.

## Introduction

### Brief History

Poland ceased to exist as an independent state following three successive partitions by neighboring empires. The First Partition (1772) began the process; the Second (1793) and Third (1795) completed the erasure of Poland from the map of Europe. For 123 years, Polish territory was divided among three powers: Russia, Prussia, and Austria.

The Congress of Vienna (1815) reorganized these territories but maintained the tripartite division. Napoleon's brief Duchy of Warsaw (1807-1815) created a short-lived Polish state, but its collapse led to the creation of Congress Poland under Russian control.

Poland regained independence in November 1918 following the collapse of all three partitioning empires in World War I.

### Why This Matters for Genealogy

The partitions created three distinct documentary traditions that persist in archives today:

1. **Language barriers**: Records were kept in Russian (Cyrillic script), German (Gothic Kurrent script), or Latin/Polish depending on the ruling power.

2. **Record formats**: Each partition developed different civil registration systems with varying start dates and information recorded.

3. **Archive locations**: Records are now split among Polish, Ukrainian, German, Russian, and other archives based on complex historical boundaries.

4. **Calendar systems**: The Russian partition used the Julian calendar (12-13 days behind Gregorian) until 1918.

5. **Name variations**: Polish surnames and place names were systematically Russified or Germanized in official records.

### Partition Boundaries

**Russian Partition (Zabór rosyjski)**: Central Poland. Includes Warsaw and extends east to the Bug River, south to Kielce, north to Płock and Łomża. The largest partition by population.

**Prussian Partition (Zabór pruski)**: Western Poland. Includes Poznań, Gdańsk, much of Pomerania, Warmia, and parts of Silesia. Extends to the German border.

**Austrian Partition (Zabór austriacki)**: Southern Poland. Galicia stretches from Kraków east through Przemyśl and Lwów (now Lviv, Ukraine) to the historical region of Podolia.

Modern voivodeships provide rough guidance:
- Mazowieckie, łódzkie, świętokrzyskie, lubelskie: primarily Russian partition
- Wielkopolskie, pomorskie, kujawsko-pomorskie, warmińsko-mazurskie: primarily Prussian partition
- Małopolskie, podkarpackie, śląskie (eastern): primarily Austrian partition

## Russian Partition (Zabór rosyjski)

### Territory Covered

Congress Poland (Królestwo Polskie), also called the Kingdom of Poland, was created at the Congress of Vienna in 1815 as a nominally autonomous state in personal union with Russia. After the failed uprisings of 1831 and 1863, autonomy was progressively eliminated.

**Modern voivodeships within the former Russian partition**:
- Mazowieckie (Warsaw region)
- Łódzkie
- Parts of świętokrzyskie (Kielce region)
- Lubelskie
- Parts of podlaskie

### Languages in Records

The language of records changed over time, reflecting increasing Russian control:

**Before 1868**:
- Church records: Latin headings with Polish or Latin content
- Civil registration (from 1808): Polish language
- Administrative documents: Polish, later Russian

**1868-1918**:
- Civil registration: Russian mandatory (Cyrillic script)
- Church records: Latin headings remained, but Russian content became common
- Catholic church records often maintained parallel Latin/Polish registers

**Common language transitions**:
- Baptism: Chrzest (Polish) → Крещение (Russian) → Baptismus (Latin)
- Marriage: Ślub (Polish) → Бракосочетание (Russian) → Matrimonium (Latin)
- Death: Zgon (Polish) → Смерть (Russian) → Obitus (Latin)

### Record Types

**Akta stanu cywilnego** (Civil registration records)

Civil registration began in the Duchy of Warsaw in 1808 under the Napoleonic Code, making this one of the earliest civil registration systems in Europe.

Format: **Akt opisowy** (narrative form). Each vital event is written as a continuous paragraph describing the event, parties, witnesses, and circumstances.

Typical contents:
- Full names of all parties
- Ages (calculated or exact birth dates)
- Occupations (zawód / занятие)
- Residences with house numbers
- Names of parents (including mother's maiden name)
- Names, ages, and occupations of witnesses

**Alegata małżeńskie** (Marriage allegata/supplements)

A distinctive feature of Russian partition records. These files contain supporting documents for marriages:
- Copies of birth records for bride and groom
- Consent letters from parents
- Certificates of publication of banns
- Death certificates of previous spouses
- Military service exemption documents

Alegata are invaluable because they often contain copies of birth records from other parishes, providing information otherwise requiring additional searches.

**Duplicate registers**

From 1826, parishes maintained duplicate registers (duplikaty / дубликаты). These were sent annually to the consistory and often survive when originals were lost.

### Archives

**Primary repositories**:

Polish State Archives (Archiwum Państwowe) hold the majority of records:
- AP Warsaw (Archiwum Główne Akt Dawnych for older records)
- AP Łódź
- AP Lublin
- AP Radom
- AP Kielce

**Online access**:
- Szukajwarchiwach.gov.pl: Polish state archives portal with digitized records
- Geneteka: Searchable index of many parishes
- FamilySearch: Microfilms of selected parishes

**Records outside Poland**:
- RGADA Moscow (Российский государственный архив древних актов): Administrative records for Congress Poland
- Some duplicate registers were retained in Russian archives

### Key Challenges

**Reading Cyrillic script**

Russian records use the pre-revolutionary Cyrillic alphabet, which includes letters no longer used in modern Russian (ѣ, і, ѳ).

Essential genealogical terms:
- Родился / Родилась (born, m/f)
- Крещён / Крещена (baptized, m/f)  
- Бракосочетание (marriage)
- Умер / Умерла (died, m/f)
- Отец (father)
- Мать (mother)
- Свидетель (witness)
- Деревня (village)
- Приход (parish)

**Russian calendar (Julian)**

The Russian Empire used the Julian calendar until February 1918. In the 19th century, the Julian calendar was 12 days behind Gregorian; in the 20th century, 13 days.

Conversion examples:
- January 15, 1870 (Julian) = January 27, 1870 (Gregorian)
- March 1, 1900 (Julian) = March 14, 1900 (Gregorian)

Church records sometimes note both dates. Civil records typically use only the Julian date.

**Russified spellings**

Polish names were transliterated into Cyrillic with varying consistency:

| Polish | Russian | Notes |
|--------|---------|-------|
| Kowalski | Ковальский | Common surname |
| Wojciech | Войцех | Given name |
| Józef | Иосиф or Осип | Biblical names often standardized |
| Władysław | Владислав | Fairly direct |
| Stanisław | Станислав | Fairly direct |
| Szczepan | Стефан | Simplified |
| Łódź | Лодзь | Place name |
| Łomża | Ломжа | Place name |

### Tips for Research

1. **Learn basic Cyrillic**: Focus on genealogical vocabulary rather than full fluency. Recognizing key terms allows navigation of records even without reading everything.

2. **Use finding aids**: Geneteka indexes include transcribed Polish names even when original records are in Russian.

3. **Check for duplicates**: If originals are damaged or missing, duplicate registers at the diocesan level may survive.

4. **Note house numbers**: Russian partition records often include house numbers (нумер дома), which can help distinguish families in the same village.

5. **Alegata are gold**: Always check for marriage supplements, which may contain birth records saving considerable research time.

## Prussian Partition (Zabór pruski)

### Territory Covered

The Prussian partition included historically Polish regions absorbed into the Kingdom of Prussia and later the German Empire.

**Major regions**:
- Wielkopolska (Greater Poland): Poznań, Gniezno, Kalisz
- Pomorze (Pomerania): Gdańsk, Bydgoszcz, Toruń
- Śląsk (Silesia): Wrocław, Opole, parts of Katowice region
- Warmia (Ermland): Olsztyn region
- Mazury (Masuria): Ethnically mixed region

**Modern voivodeships**:
- Wielkopolskie
- Pomorskie
- Kujawsko-pomorskie
- Warmińsko-mazurskie
- Parts of zachodniopomorskie
- Parts of śląskie (western)

### Languages in Records

**German** was the primary administrative language, but church records varied by denomination and period:

**Catholic church records**:
- Latin dominant through 19th century
- Polish used in some rural parishes, especially for internal records
- German increasingly common after 1870s Kulturkampf

**Protestant (Evangelical) church records**:
- German primary language
- Latin for formal entries in earlier periods

**Civil registration (from 1874)**:
- German exclusively
- Standardized Prussian forms

**Jewish records**:
- German administrative language
- Hebrew names sometimes noted alongside German forms

### Record Types

**Kirchenbücher** (Church books): Pre-1874

Catholic and Protestant churches maintained baptism, marriage, and death registers. Format varied by parish but typically included:
- Names of parties and parents
- Dates of event and (often) birth
- Residence
- Witnesses or sponsors

Catholic records often used Latin headings with German or Polish content.

**Standesamt records** (Civil registration): From October 1, 1874

Prussia introduced mandatory civil registration in 1874, predating the rest of Germany (1876).

Three separate registers:
- **Geburtenregister** (birth register)
- **Heiratsregister** (marriage register)
- **Sterberegister** (death register)

Format: Standardized forms with numbered fields. More uniform than church records but sometimes less detailed about family relationships.

**Familienbuch / Personenstandsregister**

Family registers maintained for each household, tracking vital events over time. Particularly useful for reconstructing complete family groups.

**Other valuable records**:
- **Grundbücher** (land registers): Property ownership records
- **Militärkirchenbücher** (military church books): For soldiers and their families
- **Auswandererlisten** (emigration lists): Records of those leaving for America and elsewhere

### Archives

**Polish State Archives**:
- AP Poznań: Core Wielkopolska records
- AP Gdańsk: Pomeranian records
- AP Wrocław: Silesian records
- AP Bydgoszcz: Northern Wielkopolska and Kujawy
- AP Olsztyn: Warmia and Masuria

**German archives** (for border regions and records that remained in Germany):
- Evangelisches Zentralarchiv Berlin: Protestant church records
- Various Landesarchive for specific regions

**Online resources**:
- FamilySearch: Extensive microfilm collection, especially for Pomerania and Silesia
- Ancestry: Prussian civil registration indexes
- Szukajwarchiwach.gov.pl: Polish state archives portal
- BaSIA (Baza Systemu Indeksacji Archiwalnej): Indexes for Wielkopolska region
- Poznan Project: Marriage records index for Greater Poland

### Key Challenges

**German Gothic script (Kurrentschrift)**

19th-century German records use Kurrent, a cursive blackletter script that is illegible to those trained only in modern handwriting.

Key features:
- Letters formed differently than modern equivalents
- Extensive use of ligatures
- Similar letterforms for unrelated letters (n/u, e/c)

Essential vocabulary:
- geboren (born)
- getauft (baptized)
- getraut / geheiratet (married)
- gestorben (died)
- Vater (father)
- Mutter (mother)
- Zeuge (witness)
- Taufpate / Pate (godfather)
- Taufpatin / Patin (godmother)

**Germanized place names**

Towns and villages often have both German and Polish names:

| German | Polish |
|--------|--------|
| Posen | Poznań |
| Danzig | Gdańsk |
| Thorn | Toruń |
| Bromberg | Bydgoszcz |
| Breslau | Wrocław |
| Oppeln | Opole |
| Allenstein | Olsztyn |

**Meyers Gazetteer** (Meyers Orts- und Verkehrs-Lexikon des Deutschen Reichs) is essential for identifying German place names and their administrative districts.

**Germanized surnames**

Polish surnames were often spelled phonetically in German or given German equivalents:

| Polish | Germanized |
|--------|------------|
| Kowalski | Kowalsky, Kowalske |
| Wiśniewski | Wischniewski |
| Zieliński | Sielinski, Zielinsky |
| Lis | Liß, Liss |
| Młynarz | Müller (translation) |

### Tips for Research

1. **Learn Kurrent**: Numerous online tutorials and practice sheets exist. Focus on common genealogical terms first.

2. **Use Meyers Gazetteer online**: The MEYERSGAZ project has digitized and indexed this essential resource for German place name identification.

3. **Check BaSIA and Poznan Project**: These volunteer projects have indexed hundreds of thousands of records from Wielkopolska specifically.

4. **Catholic vs. Protestant**: Determine religious affiliation early, as records are held separately. Mixed marriages required dispensations that generate additional documentation.

5. **1874 transition**: Be aware that church records continue after 1874 but civil registration becomes the primary legal source.

6. **Border changes**: Many Prussian towns are now in Poland, but some records remained in Germany. Check both Polish and German archives for border areas.

## Austrian Partition (Zabór austriacki)

### Territory Covered

The Austrian partition, officially known as the Kingdom of Galicia and Lodomeria (Königreich Galizien und Lodomerien), stretched across southern Poland and western Ukraine.

**Major regions**:
- Galicja Zachodnia (Western Galicia): Kraków, Tarnów, Rzeszów
- Galicja Wschodnia (Eastern Galicia): Przemyśl, Lwów (Lviv), Stanisławów (Ivano-Frankivsk)

**Modern Polish voivodeships**:
- Małopolskie (Lesser Poland)
- Podkarpackie
- Parts of śląskie (eastern)

**Modern Ukrainian oblasts** (for eastern Galicia):
- Lviv Oblast
- Ivano-Frankivsk Oblast
- Ternopil Oblast

### Languages in Records

**Latin** was the dominant language in Catholic church records throughout the Austrian period, making these records more accessible to researchers than Russian or German partition records.

**Church records**:
- Latin primary language for headings and standard phrases
- Polish used for descriptive content, especially in western Galicia
- Ukrainian (Ruthenian) in Greek Catholic parishes of eastern Galicia

**Administrative records**:
- German official language of the empire
- Polish increasingly used after 1867 autonomy
- Latin in church administrative documents

**Jewish records**:
- German or Polish administrative language
- Hebrew given names often noted
- Yiddish names sometimes transcribed phonetically

### Record Types

**Matryki kościelne** (Church registers)

Austrian Galicia relied primarily on church registration; civil registration was not introduced until 1940s.

**Format**: Unlike the narrative Russian style, Galician records used **tabular format** (format tabelaryczny) with pre-printed columns:
- Name columns for child, parents, godparents
- Date columns for birth and baptism
- Residence column
- Occupation column
- Comments column for legitimacy, notes

**Standard columns in baptismal registers**:
- Rok, miesiąc, dzień (year, month, day)
- Imię ochrzczonego (name of baptized)
- Płeć (sex)
- Czy ślubne (legitimate/illegitimate)
- Rodzice: imię, nazwisko, stan (parents: name, surname, status)
- Chrzestni: imię, nazwisko (godparents: name, surname)
- Uwagi (remarks)

**Józefińskie pomiary katastralne** (Josephinian cadastre)

Land surveys conducted under Joseph II (1780s) documented property ownership and are valuable for locating ancestors and understanding village structure.

**Franciscejskie katastry** (Franciscan cadastre)

Updated cadastral surveys from the mid-19th century with more detailed maps.

**Księgi metrykalne** for various denominations:
- Roman Catholic (majority population)
- Greek Catholic (eastern Galicia, Ukrainian population)
- Jewish (significant communities throughout)
- Protestant (smaller communities)

### Archives

**Polish State Archives**:
- AP Kraków: Western Galicia core
- AP Przemyśl: Southeastern regions
- AP Rzeszów: Central Galicia
- Various branch archives (Tarnów, Nowy Sącz)

**Ukrainian archives** (for eastern Galicia):
- CDIAU Lviv (Центральний державний історичний архів України, Львів): Central State Historical Archive of Ukraine
- DALO (Державний архів Львівської області): Lviv Oblast State Archive
- Various oblast archives for Ivano-Frankivsk, Ternopil

**Online resources**:
- Szukajwarchiwach.gov.pl: Polish state archives
- FamilySearch: Significant Galician collections
- Geneteka: Growing coverage for western Galicia
- Gesher Galicia: Comprehensive Jewish records project

### Key Challenges

**Latin abbreviations**

Church records use extensive abbreviations for common terms:

| Abbreviation | Latin | Meaning |
|--------------|-------|---------|
| b.m. | bonae memoriae | of good memory (deceased) |
| d.d. | de domo | maiden name |
| fil. | filius/filia | son/daughter |
| leg. | legitimus/legitima | legitimate |
| ill. | illegitimus | illegitimate |
| ss. / ss.rr. | sacramentis | with sacraments |
| h.l. | huius loci | of this place |
| N.N. | nomen nescio | name unknown |
| ob. | obiit | died |
| aet. | aetatis | of age |
| bapt. | baptisatus/a | baptized |
| cop. | copulatus/a | married |
| sep. | sepultus/a | buried |

**Records split between Poland and Ukraine**

The post-WWI and post-WWII borders divided Galicia. Researchers with eastern Galician ancestry must navigate:
- Different archive systems
- Different languages (Ukrainian, not Polish)
- Different online resources
- Potential access restrictions

**Complex administrative divisions**

Austrian administrative structure changed over time:
- **Bezirk** (district): Administrative unit
- **Kreis** (circle): Larger regional unit, used in earlier period
- **Gemeinde** (gmina): Municipality
- **Pfarrei** (parafia): Parish

Records may be organized by any of these units depending on period and archive.

### Tips for Research

1. **Geneteka for western Galicia**: Coverage continues to expand; check indexes before ordering archival copies.

2. **Gesher Galicia essential for Jewish families**: This project has indexed vital records, census records, and other sources specifically for Galician Jewish communities.

3. **Austrian cadastral maps**: Available online through AGAD and other sources. Invaluable for locating villages and understanding property relationships.

4. **Greek Catholic records**: Eastern Galicia records may be in Ukrainian archives and use Cyrillic script; Greek Catholic parishes served the Ukrainian population.

5. **Latin is a feature, not a bug**: Standard Latin phrases are consistent across parishes, making records more accessible once learned than German Kurrent or Russian Cyrillic.

6. **Check for Josephinian numbering**: House numbers assigned during the Josephinian cadastre persist in later records and help identify specific households.

## Identifying Your Ancestor's Partition

### By Modern Location

The simplest method is to identify the modern Polish voivodeship:

**Russian Partition**:
- Mazowieckie
- Łódzkie
- Lubelskie
- Świętokrzyskie (most)
- Podlaskie (most)

**Prussian Partition**:
- Wielkopolskie
- Pomorskie
- Kujawsko-pomorskie
- Warmińsko-mazurskie
- Zachodniopomorskie
- Lubuskie
- Dolnośląskie (most)
- Opolskie

**Austrian Partition**:
- Małopolskie
- Podkarpackie
- Parts of śląskie (east)

### Border Regions

Several areas changed hands between partitions or were divided:

**Kraków**: Free City of Kraków (1815-1846), then Austrian
**Silesia**: Divided between Prussia and Austria; Upper Silesia was particularly complex
**Podlasie**: Split between Russian and Prussian partitions
**Kujawy**: Boundary zone between Russian and Prussian partitions

### Using Gazetteers

When the modern location is unclear:
1. **Słownik geograficzny Królestwa Polskiego** (Geographical Dictionary of the Kingdom of Poland): 15-volume encyclopedia with detailed entries for towns and villages
2. **Meyers Gazetteer**: For Prussian partition locations
3. **Gemeindelexikon für die Reichslande Elsaß-Lothringen**: Not directly applicable but similar resources exist for Austrian territories

### Quick Tests

**Russian partition indicators**:
- Civil records from 1808 onward
- Narrative format records
- Russian language after 1868
- Julian calendar dates

**Prussian partition indicators**:
- Civil registration from 1874
- German language throughout
- Gothic Kurrent script
- Protestant populations significant

**Austrian partition indicators**:
- Church records primary (no civil registration until 1940s)
- Tabular format
- Latin with Polish content
- Records potentially in Ukrainian archives (eastern areas)

## Cross-Partition Migrations

### Movement Between Partitions

Despite borders, significant population movement occurred:

**Economic migration**:
- Agricultural workers moved seasonally between partitions
- Industrial workers migrated to growing cities regardless of partition
- Łódź (Russian partition) drew workers from all three partitions

**Marriage across borders**:
- Families near partition boundaries often intermarried
- Marriage records may reference birthplaces in different partitions
- Follow-up research requires familiarity with multiple record systems

**Military service**:
- Men served in Russian, German, or Austrian armies depending on partition
- Military records are held separately from civil archives
- Border regions saw conscription into multiple armies over generations

### Seasonal Workers (Sachsengänger)

Large numbers of Poles from the Russian and Austrian partitions migrated seasonally to work in Prussian agriculture and industry. These workers:
- May appear in Prussian records despite being residents of other partitions
- Sometimes settled permanently in Prussia
- Left paper trails in multiple archives

### Tracking Cross-Partition Families

1. **Note birthplaces carefully**: Marriage and death records often list birthplaces, revealing prior residence in different partitions.

2. **Follow the paper**: A marriage in the Russian partition may include alegata with birth certificates from the Austrian partition.

3. **Language changes**: An ancestor born in Galicia (Latin records), married in Congress Poland (Polish/Russian records), with children baptized in Poznań (German records) requires three different paleographic skills.

4. **Use indexes across partitions**: Projects like Geneteka index records from multiple partitions; a broad search may reveal connections.

## Timeline of Key Events

### 1772: First Partition
Russia, Prussia, and Austria seize approximately 30% of Polish territory. Prussia gains Royal Prussia including Gdańsk corridor; Austria gains Galicia; Russia gains eastern territories.

### 1793: Second Partition
Prussia and Russia partition additional territory following the Four-Year Sejm reforms. Prussia gains Greater Poland including Poznań; Russia gains central eastern regions.

### 1795: Third Partition
Poland ceases to exist. Austria, Prussia, and Russia absorb remaining territory. Warsaw goes to Prussia initially.

### 1807: Duchy of Warsaw Created
Napoleon establishes the Duchy of Warsaw from Prussian partition lands. Civil registration introduced under Napoleonic Code.

### 1809: Duchy of Warsaw Expanded
Austrian territory (western Galicia including Kraków) added to Duchy following Austrian defeat.

### 1815: Congress of Vienna
Duchy of Warsaw partitioned. Congress Poland (Kingdom of Poland) created under Russian suzerainty. Kraków becomes a Free City. Prussia regains Poznań.

### 1831: November Uprising
Failed Polish uprising against Russian rule. Congress Poland autonomy reduced. Russian repression increases.

### 1846: Kraków Incorporated
Free City of Kraków absorbed into Austrian Galicia following failed uprising.

### 1863-1864: January Uprising
Major uprising in Russian partition fails. Brutal Russian repression follows. Administrative autonomy eliminated.

### 1867: Austrian Autonomy Granted
Galicia gains significant autonomy within Austro-Hungarian dual monarchy. Polish language rights expanded. University of Kraków and Lwów use Polish.

### 1868: Russian Language Mandated
Russian becomes mandatory language for civil registration in Congress Poland. Russification intensifies.

### 1871: German Empire Proclaimed
Prussian partition becomes part of unified German Empire. Kulturkampf begins, affecting Catholic Church.

### 1874: Prussian Civil Registration
Mandatory civil registration (Standesamt) begins October 1, 1874 in Prussia.

### 1876: German Civil Registration
Civil registration extends to entire German Empire.

### 1914: World War I Begins
All three empires enter the war. Polish soldiers fight on multiple sides.

### 1918: Poland Regains Independence
Collapse of all three empires. Polish Republic proclaimed November 11, 1918. Calendar unified to Gregorian.

### 1920: Border Wars
Polish-Soviet War and other conflicts establish eastern borders. Some Galician territory remains with Poland; other parts join Soviet Ukraine.

### 1939-1945: World War II
German and Soviet occupation. Borders redrawn again after war. Eastern Galicia becomes part of Soviet Ukraine (now independent Ukraine).
