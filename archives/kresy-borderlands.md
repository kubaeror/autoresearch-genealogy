# Kresy (Polish Eastern Borderlands)

Genealogical research for families from the former Polish Eastern Borderlands requires navigating multiple national archives, shifting borders, and records in several languages.

## Introduction

### What are the Kresy?

Kresy (pronounced KREH-sy) means "borderlands" in Polish. The term refers to the eastern territories of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth and interwar Poland (1918–1939) that were annexed by the Soviet Union in 1939 and are now part of Ukraine, Belarus, and Lithuania.

For centuries, these lands were home to a multiethnic population: Poles, Ukrainians, Belarusians, Lithuanians, Jews, and smaller communities of Germans, Armenians, and Tatars. The region's complexity stems from:

- **Shifting sovereignty**: These territories changed hands repeatedly between Poland, Russia, Austria, and the Soviet Union
- **Population transfers**: After 1944, most ethnic Poles were forcibly "repatriated" to postwar Poland's new borders
- **Multiple record systems**: Civil and religious records exist in Polish, Russian, Ukrainian, Latin, German, and Yiddish
- **Dispersed families**: After 1945, Kresy families scattered across Poland, the USSR, and emigration destinations

### Historical Context

The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth (1569–1795) once stretched from the Baltic to the Black Sea. The eastern reaches included cities like Wilno (Vilnius), Lwów (Lviv), and Brześć (Brest). After the partitions (1772–1795), these lands fell under Russian or Austrian rule.

Following World War I, Poland regained independence and reclaimed much of the eastern borderlands through the Polish-Soviet War (1919–1921). The Treaty of Riga (1921) established Poland's eastern border until 1939.

The Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact (1939) assigned these territories to the Soviet Union. After 1945, the borders became permanent, and 1.5 to 2 million ethnic Poles were displaced westward.

## Geographic Scope

### Territories Now in Ukraine

**Eastern Galicia** (Galicja Wschodnia / Східна Галичина):
- Major cities: Lwów/Lviv (Львів), Stanisławów/Ivano-Frankivsk (Івано-Франківськ), Tarnopol/Ternopil (Тернопіль)
- Austrian rule 1772–1918, Polish 1918–1939
- Strong Greek Catholic (Uniate) presence alongside Roman Catholic

**Volhynia** (Wołyń / Волинь):
- Major cities: Łuck/Lutsk (Луцьк), Równe/Rivne (Рівне), Kowel/Kovel (Ковель)
- Russian rule 1795–1918, Polish 1918–1939
- Site of the 1943 Volhynia massacres; many records destroyed

### Territories Now in Belarus

**Grodno Region** (Ziemia Grodzieńska / Гродзенская вобласць):
- Major cities: Grodno (Гродна), Lida (Ліда), Nowogródek/Navahrudak (Навагрудак)

**Brest Region** (Polesie / Берасцейская вобласць):
- Major cities: Brześć/Brest (Брэст), Pińsk (Пінск), Kobryń (Кобрын)

### Territories Now in Lithuania

**Vilnius Region** (Wileńszczyzna / Vilniaus kraštas):
- Major city: Wilno/Vilnius
- Polish administration 1920–1939
- Complex Polish-Lithuanian relations; records in both languages

## Ukrainian Archives

### Central State Historical Archive of Ukraine in Lviv (CDIAU Lviv)

**Ukrainian**: Центральний державний історичний архів України у Львові (ЦДІАУ у Львові)

- **URL**: https://archives.gov.ua/ (main portal); https://cdial.archives.gov.ua/ (Lviv branch)
- **Holdings**: Records from Austrian Galicia (1772–1918) and interwar Poland (1918–1939)
  - Greek Catholic (Uniate) parish registers
  - Roman Catholic parish registers
  - Jewish community records (metrical books, community registers)
  - Civil registration records
  - Land and property records
  - Gymnasium and university records
- **Coverage**: Eastern Galicia, including Lwów, Stanisławów, Tarnopol voivodeships
- **Access**: Limited online availability. Some records indexed on Geneteka under "Ukraina" tab. On-site research possible with advance arrangements.

**How to request records**:
1. Identify the parish or civil registration office (USC) for your ancestor's village
2. Use finding aids on the CDIAU website to locate the fond (collection) and opis (inventory)
3. Submit a written request (email or postal) specifying the fond, opis, and delo (file) numbers
4. Include ancestor's name, approximate dates, and village name
5. Fees apply for searches and copies; payment methods vary

### Regional State Archives

**DALO (Derzhavnyi arkhiv Lvivskoi oblasti)**: Lviv Oblast regional records
- **URL**: https://archivelviv.gov.ua/
- **Holdings**: 20th century records, some 19th century civil records

**DAZO (Derzhavnyi arkhiv Zakarpatskoї oblasti)**: Transcarpathia (former Subcarpathian Ruthenia)
- Historically Hungarian, not Polish; included for completeness

**DADO (Derzhavnyi arkhiv Donetskoi oblasti)**: Eastern Ukraine; limited Kresy relevance

**DATO (Derzhavnyi arkhiv Ternopilskoi oblasti)**: Tarnopol region records
- **URL**: https://archivete.gov.ua/

**DAIVO (Derzhavnyi arkhiv Ivano-Frankivskoi oblasti)**: Stanisławów region records

**Online availability**: Ukrainian archives have begun digitization, but online access remains limited. FamilySearch has microfilmed some collections, accessible at FamilySearch Centers.

## Belarusian Archives

**Belarusian**: Нацыянальны гістарычны архіў Беларусі (НГАБ)

- **URL**: https://niab.by/ (main archive); https://archives.gov.by/ (portal)
- **Holdings**:

**NIAB Branches**:
- NIAB Grodno: Records specific to Grodno oblast
- NIAB Minsk: Main collection

**Request process**: Similar to Ukrainian archives. Written requests with specific details. Fees for research services.

### Geneteka Coverage for Belarus

**Limitations**:
- Coverage is significantly sparser than core Polish voivodeships

**Search tips**:
- Search all three tabs (Ukraina, Białoruś, Litwa) plus neighboring Polish voivodeships
- Kresy families often have relatives who remained in or moved to central Poland
- Use both Polish and localized spellings of place names

## Population Transfers and Repatriation

### 1939–1941: Soviet Occupation

Following the September 1939 invasion, the Soviet Union annexed eastern Poland. Subsequent events:

- **Mass deportations** (1940–1941): Four major waves of deportations to Siberia, Kazakhstan, and other Soviet regions. An estimated 320,000 to 1 million Poles were deported.
- **Targets**: Polish civil servants, military families, foresters, settlers, "class enemies"
- **Records**: Deportation lists, NKVD files; some accessible through Russian and Kazakh archives

### 1944–1946: "Repatriation" to Poland

After the Soviet reconquest and the Yalta Agreement (1945), ethnic Poles were pressured or forced to leave the Kresy for Poland's new borders:

- **"Repatriation" agreements**: Between the Polish Committee of National Liberation (PKWN) and Soviet republics (1944–1946)
- **Numbers**: 1.5 to 2 million Poles relocated westward
- **Destinations**: Recovered Territories (formerly German Silesia, Pomerania, East Prussia), central Poland

**Tracking relocated ancestors**:

1. **Identify the village of origin** in Kresy through family knowledge or Polish records
2. **Check repatriation transport lists**: These record the village of origin, family members, destination in Poland
3. **Search destination parish records**: Many repatriates appear in Polish civil or church records from 1945 onward
4. **Contact Polish state archives**: Repatriation records are held in regional archives (e.g., Wrocław for repatriates to Lower Silesia)

### Where Repatriation Records Are Kept

| Repository | Holdings |
|------------|----------|
| Polish state archives (regional) | Transport lists, resettlement records by destination region |
| Archiwum Akt Nowych (AAN), Warsaw | Central repatriation administration records |
| Local gmina (commune) offices | Registration of incoming repatriates |
| Parish records | Baptism and marriage records noting Kresy origins |

## Key Resources for Deportations and Repression

### Indeks Represjonowanych (Index of Repressed)

- **URL**: https://indeksrepresjonowanych.pl/
- **Holdings**: Database of Polish citizens repressed by Soviet authorities (1939–1956)
- **Content**: Names, dates, places of deportation, fates
- **Searchable**: Yes, by name

### Ośrodek KARTA

- **URL**: https://karta.org.pl/
- **Holdings**: Archives documenting Soviet repression of Poles
- **Programs**:
  - Indeks Represjonowanych (hosted here)
  - Eastern Archive (Archiwum Wschodnie): oral histories, documents from Kresy
- **Access**: Research services available; some collections digitized

### IPN (Instytut Pamięci Narodowej / Institute of National Remembrance)

- **URL**: https://ipn.gov.pl/
- **Holdings**: Communist-era security service files, WWII documentation
- **Relevance**: Files on deportees, political prisoners, wartime victims
- **Access**: Individual records requests; some databases online

### Kresy-Siberia Foundation

- **URL**: https://krfrst.naszapolska.pl/ (Note: website availability varies)
- **Mission**: Documentation of Polish deportees to Siberia and Central Asia
- **Holdings**: Survivor testimonies, deportation research, family tracing assistance
- **Resources**: Virtual Museum, educational materials

## Research Strategies for Kresy Families

### Step-by-Step Approach

1. **Start with what you know in Poland**
   - Interview living relatives
   - Gather documents: birth certificates, photos, letters
   - Check Polish civil records for post-1945 registrations noting Kresy birthplaces

2. **Identify the exact village in Kresy**
   - Use Słownik Geograficzny Królestwa Polskiego (19th century Polish geographic dictionary)
   - Check historical maps on Mapster (https://mapster.pl/)
   - Cross-reference place names in multiple languages

3. **Determine which modern country contains the village**
   - Use JewishGen's ShtetlSeeker or WGFF Gazetteer
   - Note: some villages no longer exist or have been renamed

4. **Check Geneteka for indexed records**
   - Search Ukraina, Białoruś, and Litwa tabs
   - Also search neighboring Polish voivodeships

5. **Search foreign archives**
   - CDIAU (Ukraine) for Galicia and Volhynia
   - NIAB (Belarus) for Grodno and Brest regions
   - LVIA (Lithuania) for Vilnius region
   - Consider hiring local researchers if language or access is a barrier

6. **Look for repatriation and deportation records**
   - Check Indeks Represjonowanych
   - Contact Polish regional archives based on known post-1945 residence
   - Search IPN databases

7. **Document everything**
   - Log negative results ("Searched CDIAU fond X, found nothing")
   - Track which archives, collections, and years have been checked
   - Note language and script of each record type

## Common Challenges

### Place Name Changes

Villages, towns, and cities in Kresy have names in multiple languages, and official names changed with each regime:

| Period | Example (Lwów) |
|--------|----------------|
| Austrian (1772–1918) | Lemberg (German), Lwów (Polish) |
| Polish (1918–1939) | Lwów |
| Soviet (1939–1941, 1944–1991) | Львов (Lvov) |
| Ukrainian (1991–present) | Львів (Lviv) |

**Tools for place name identification**:
- Słownik Geograficzny Królestwa Polskiego (via Polona.pl)
- JewishGen ShtetlSeeker
- Mapster.pl historical maps
- Wikipedia (check all language versions)

### Records in Multiple Languages and Scripts

| Language | Script | Common In |
|----------|--------|-----------|
| Polish | Latin | Church records (Catholic), Austrian civil records |
| Latin | Latin | Catholic church records (pre-1868 baptisms, marriages) |
| Russian | Cyrillic | Civil records under Russian rule (1795–1918), Soviet-era |
| Ukrainian | Cyrillic | Greek Catholic records, modern Ukrainian archives |
| German | Gothic/Fraktur | Austrian administrative records |
| Yiddish/Hebrew | Hebrew | Jewish community records |

**Practical tips**:
- Learn to recognize key words in each language (birth, death, marriage, son, daughter)
- Use Cyrillic transliteration tools
- FamilySearch Wiki has reading guides for each script and language

### Family Dispersal After 1945

A single Kresy family may have ended up in:
- Postwar Poland (repatriation)
- Soviet Union (deportation to Siberia, Kazakhstan)
- Western Europe (displaced persons camps, emigration)
- Americas, Australia (postwar emigration)

**Strategy**: Research all branches. Emigration records (ship manifests, naturalization papers) often contain exact birthplace information.

### Damaged and Destroyed Records

- **Volhynia massacres (1943)**: Many villages burned, records lost
- **Soviet occupation (1939–1941)**: Some church records confiscated or destroyed
- **WWII combat**: Archive buildings damaged in Lwów, Wilno, and other cities
- **Postwar neglect**: Some records deteriorated under Soviet administration

Check multiple repositories; duplicate records may exist in diocesan or state archives.

## Kresy-Specific Record Types

### Greek Catholic (Uniate) Church Records

The Greek Catholic Church (Українська греко-католицька церква) was dominant among Ukrainians in Eastern Galicia. Records resemble Roman Catholic registers but may be in:
- Old Church Slavonic
- Ukrainian
- Latin (later periods)

Holdings: CDIAU Lviv, diocesan archives, some FamilySearch microfilms.

### Orthodox Church Records

Prevalent in Volhynia and territories under Russian rule. Records in:
- Russian (Cyrillic)
- Old Church Slavonic

Holdings: Ukrainian and Belarusian state archives, Russian archives for some periods.

### Jewish Records

Kresy had large Jewish populations before the Holocaust. Record types:
- **Metrical books**: Births, marriages, deaths registered with Jewish communities
- **Kahal records**: Community administrative records
- **Revision lists** (ревизские сказки): Russian census-like documents

**Key resources**:
- JewishGen (https://www.jewishgen.org/)
- Gesher Galicia (https://www.geshergalicia.org/)
- Yad Vashem databases
- YIVO Archives

### Multiethnic Communities

Many Kresy towns had mixed populations. A single village might have:
- Roman Catholic parish (Poles)
- Greek Catholic parish (Ukrainians)
- Orthodox parish (Ukrainians, Belarusians)
- Jewish community (kehilla)
- Sometimes Lutheran or other Protestant communities

**Strategy**: If ancestors intermarried or converted, check all relevant religious records.
