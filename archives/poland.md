# Poland

## Introduction

Polish genealogy presents unique challenges and opportunities. The records are extensive, often surviving back to the 16th century for some parishes, but researching them requires understanding a complex historical and administrative landscape.

Three factors make Polish genealogy particularly challenging:

**The Partitions (1795–1918)**: Poland ceased to exist as an independent nation for 123 years, divided among Russia, Prussia, and Austria. Each partition maintained records in different languages, using different administrative systems, and applying different legal requirements. A single family living in the same village might have records in Latin, Polish, Russian, and German depending on the era.

**Border Changes**: Poland's borders shifted dramatically after World War I and again after World War II. Towns that were "Polish" before 1795 became Russian, Prussian, or Austrian; then Polish again after 1918; then Soviet or German after 1939; and finally Polish (or Soviet) after 1945. Knowing which country administered a location at a given time determines where to find records.

**Multiple Languages**: A single parish register might contain entries in Latin (Catholic church records), Polish (post-1808 civil registration in some areas), Russian (post-1868 in Congress Poland), or German (Prussian areas). Researchers must navigate all of these.

The good news: Polish genealogical societies have digitized and indexed millions of records, making them increasingly accessible online.

## Key Online Databases

### Geneteka

- **URL**: https://geneteka.genealodzy.pl/
- **Coverage**: 65+ million indexed vital records (births, marriages, deaths)
- **Cost**: Free
- **Searchable**: Yes (surname, parish, event type, date range, spouse surname)
- **AI accessibility**: Excellent. Results are structured HTML tables that can be parsed programmatically.
- **Languages**: Polish interface (some English elements)

**What It Contains**:
Geneteka is the most important starting point for Polish genealogy. It is a collaborative index maintained by the Polskie Towarzystwo Genealogiczne (Polish Genealogical Society). Volunteers transcribe records from parish registers and civil registration offices, creating searchable indexes. The database does not contain images of original documents, only extracted data.

**Coverage by Voivodeship**:
Coverage varies significantly. Some voivodeships (województwa) have near-complete coverage for the 19th century; others have major gaps. Always check the "Stan indeksacji" (indexing status) page for your target region.

Well-indexed voivodeships include: Mazowieckie, Łódzkie, Kujawsko-Pomorskie, Wielkopolskie (partial, see also BaSIA).

Poorly-indexed voivodeships include: Podkarpackie, Lubelskie (partial, see also Lubgens), Małopolskie.

**Search Strategies**:

1. **Start broad**: Search your surname across all voivodeships ("Wszystkie" option) to see where the name concentrates. This reveals the family's likely region of origin.

2. **Surname variants**: Polish records contain many spelling variations. Search for phonetic equivalents: Kowalczyk, Kowalczik, Kowalczuk; Mazurek, Mazurkiewicz; Wojciechowski, Wojciechowsky.

3. **Cross-reference spouses**: Marriage records list both spouses. If you know a wife's maiden name, search for marriages matching both surnames in the same parish and date range.

4. **Use indirect searches ("Szukaj w innych")**: Geneteka offers "Szukaj w innych" (search in others), which finds your surname appearing as a witness, godparent, or spouse's parent. This reveals family connections that do not appear in direct vital records.

5. **Note the "Uwagi" column**: The notes column often contains the archive reference number (sygnatura) needed to locate the original document in Szukaj w Archiwach.

**Limitations**:
Geneteka is an index, not a source. Always verify indexed data against original documents. Transcription errors occur. Some records are indexed without the original document being digitized anywhere.

### Szukaj w Archiwach (Search in Archives)

- **URL**: https://www.szukajwarchiwach.gov.pl/
- **Coverage**: Digitized holdings from all Polish state archives (Archiwa Państwowe)
- **Cost**: Free
- **Searchable**: Browse hierarchy; limited keyword search for finding aids
- **AI accessibility**: Navigation pages are HTML; actual records are scanned images requiring multimodal/OCR for reading
- **Languages**: Polish (some English interface)

**What It Contains**:
Szukaj w Archiwach is the official portal of the Polish state archives. It provides access to digitized parish registers (księgi metrykalne), civil registration records (akta stanu cywilnego), notarial records, court records, land records, and much more. This is where you view the original documents that Geneteka indexes.

**Navigation Hierarchy**:
Records are organized by: Archive (Archiwum) → Fond/Collection (Zespół) → Series (Seria) → Unit (Jednostka) → Scans.

Example path: Archiwum Państwowe w Łodzi → Akta stanu cywilnego Parafii Rzymskokatolickiej w Bełchatowie → Urodzenia 1826 → (scans)

**How to Find Records**:

1. Identify the parish (parafia) or civil registration office (Urząd Stanu Cywilnego, USC) for your ancestor's village.
2. Determine which state archive holds records for that region.
3. Navigate to the relevant fond and locate the year/event type you need.
4. Browse scanned images. Images are JPEG; quality varies.

**Types of Records Available**:
- Akta stanu cywilnego (civil registration): births, marriages, deaths from 1808 onward (Russian partition) or later (other partitions)
- Księgi metrykalne (parish registers): baptisms, marriages, burials from Catholic, Lutheran, Orthodox, and some Jewish parishes
- Alegata (marriage supplements): attached documents such as birth certificates, parental consent, banns
- Notarial records: land sales, wills, contracts
- Spisy ludności (population registers): occasional censuses

**Limitations**:
Not all archives have digitized their holdings. Even within digitized collections, some volumes may be missing or damaged. The search function finds archival descriptions, not individual names within records.

### Metryki.genealodzy.pl

- **URL**: https://metryki.genealodzy.pl/
- **Coverage**: 12.7 million scanned pages from parish and civil registers
- **Cost**: Free
- **Searchable**: Browse by voivodeship and parish
- **AI accessibility**: Browse-only images; requires multimodal for reading
- **Languages**: Polish

**What It Contains**:
This is a parallel digitization project run by the Polish Genealogical Society. It contains scans of parish registers that may not appear in Szukaj w Archiwach, particularly from diocesan archives (which are separate from state archives). Coverage overlaps with Szukaj w Archiwach in some areas but is complementary in others.

**Organization**:
Records are organized by voivodeship, then by powiat (county), then by parish, then by record type and year.

**When to Use**:
Check here when Szukaj w Archiwach does not have scans for your parish, or when you want to verify coverage. Some parishes appear in Metryki but not in Szukaj w Archiwach because the originals remain in diocesan archives rather than state archives.

### BaSIA (Baza Systemu Indeksacji Archiwalnej)

- **URL**: https://www.basia.famula.pl/
- **Coverage**: 6.6 million indexed records from Wielkopolska region
- **Cost**: Free
- **Searchable**: Yes (surname, given name, event type, parish, date range)
- **AI accessibility**: Excellent. Structured search results. Links to original scans.
- **Languages**: Polish

**What It Contains**:
BaSIA is the primary database for the Wielkopolska (Greater Poland) region, covering the western part of Poland centered on Poznań. It is a partnership between genealogical volunteers and the Archiwum Państwowe w Poznaniu. Records include vital records (births, marriages, deaths) from both Catholic and Protestant parishes.

**Key Features**:
- Indexes are linked to scans of original documents when available
- Includes Evangelical (Lutheran) records, which are extensive in this region due to Prussian influence
- Better coverage for German-language records than Geneteka

**Search Tips**:
Use BaSIA alongside Geneteka for Wielkopolska research. Some records appear in one but not the other. BaSIA's interface allows searching by both Polish and German spellings of place names.

### Poznań Project

- **URL**: https://poznan-project.psnc.pl/
- **Coverage**: 1 million+ marriage records from Wielkopolska, 1800–1899
- **Cost**: Free
- **Searchable**: Yes (surname, parish)
- **AI accessibility**: Excellent. Structured search results.
- **Languages**: English interface; record data in Polish/Latin/German

**What It Contains**:
The Poznań Project is a specialized index of 19th-century marriage records from the Wielkopolska region. Marriage records are particularly valuable because they typically list: bride and groom's names, ages, birthplaces, occupations, residences, parents' names, and witnesses.

**Primary Use**:
Finding maiden names. If you know an ancestor's married name but not her maiden name, the Poznań Project can locate the marriage record, which lists both families.

**Coverage**:
Primarily Catholic parishes; some Evangelical parishes. Coverage extends to areas that were part of Wielkopolska in the 19th century, which differs slightly from modern boundaries.

### Pomorskie Towarzystwo Genealogiczne (PTG)

- **URL**: https://www.ptg.gda.pl/
- **Coverage**: Pomeranian region indexes (Gdańsk, Elbląg, and surrounding areas)
- **Cost**: Free
- **Searchable**: Multiple databases with different search interfaces
- **AI accessibility**: Moderate. Some structured data; some PDF/image resources.
- **Languages**: Polish

**What It Contains**:

**Vital Records Indexes**: Transcribed birth, marriage, and death records from Pomeranian parishes, particularly useful for the formerly Prussian areas where records are in German.

**Cemetery Indexes**: Transcriptions of gravestone inscriptions from Pomeranian cemeteries, including many that were destroyed or overgrown.

**Memorial and War Records**: Indexes of war memorials, casualty lists, and related documents.

**When to Use**:
Essential for research in the Gdańsk/Danzig region, Kashubia, and areas along the Baltic coast. This region has distinct ethnic and religious complexity (Polish, German, Kashubian; Catholic, Lutheran, Mennonite).

### Lubgens

- **URL**: https://www.lubgens.eu/
- **Coverage**: Lublin voivodeship indexes and research community
- **Cost**: Free
- **Searchable**: Forum-based; some structured indexes
- **AI accessibility**: Limited. Forum posts and some Excel/PDF indexes.
- **Languages**: Polish

**What It Contains**:
Lubgens is both a database and a community. The site hosts transcribed indexes of vital records from the Lublin region (southeastern Poland), along with an active forum where researchers share findings and request transcriptions.

**Community Transcription**:
One of Lubgens' most valuable features is the ability to request transcriptions. If you find a reference to a record but cannot read the original, forum members may be able to help transcribe it.

**Limitations**:
Coverage is inconsistent. Some parishes are well-indexed; others have no coverage. The forum format makes systematic searching difficult.

---

### Mapa Nazwisk (Surname Map)

- **URL**: https://www.mapanazwisk.eu/en/
- **Coverage**: Modern distribution of all Polish surnames
- **Cost**: Free
- **Searchable**: Yes (by surname)
- **AI accessibility**: Excellent. Structured results with counts by powiat.
- **Languages**: Polish and English

**What It Contains**:
Mapa Nazwisk displays the geographic distribution of any Polish surname based on 1990 and 2002 census data. It shows how many people with a given surname live in each powiat (county).

**Primary Use**:
Identifying the probable region of origin for a surname. Surnames often cluster in specific areas, reflecting historical settlement patterns. A surname concentrated in one or two powiats suggests the family likely originated there.

**Example**:
Searching "Szczepański" shows heavy concentration in central Poland (Mazowieckie, Łódzkie), moderate presence in Wielkopolska, and sparse distribution elsewhere. This suggests searching those regions first.

**Limitations**:
Modern distribution reflects 20th-century migration patterns. Industrialization, World War II displacement, and post-war border changes all affected where families live today. Use as a starting point, not definitive proof of origin.

## Regional and Specialized Databases

Beyond the major databases, many voivodeships and specialized organizations maintain their own resources:

**Galicia**:
- Gesher Galicia (geshergalicia.org): Records from the former Austrian partition, now split between Poland and Ukraine. Particularly strong for Jewish records.
- Cadastral maps and tax records from Austrian administration

**Silesia**:
- Landesarchiv Baden-Württemberg (for Catholic records microfilmed before WWII)
- Silesian genealogical societies

**Kresy (Eastern Borderlands)**:
- Records from areas now in Belarus, Ukraine, and Lithuania
- AGAD in Warsaw holds many Kresy collections

**Jewish Records**:
- JRI-Poland (jri-poland.org): Jewish vital records from Polish state archives
- Yad Vashem: Holocaust-era records
- JewishGen: Multiple databases for Polish Jewish genealogy

## Polish Naming Conventions

### Przydomki (Hereditary Nicknames)

Przydomki (singular: przydomek) are hereditary nicknames attached to a family's surname. They are a uniquely Polish phenomenon arising from the prevalence of certain surnames.

**Why They Exist**:
Poland has extremely common surnames. The top 10 surnames account for a significant percentage of the population. In rural villages where many families shared the same surname, a secondary identifier was needed.

**How They Work**:
A family would be known as "Kowalski zwany Prusak" (Kowalski called Prusak) or "Kowalski alias Skrzypek" (Kowalski alias Skrzypek). The przydomek could derive from:
- A personal characteristic (Gruby = fat, Mały = small)
- An occupation (Skrzypek = fiddler, Kowal = smith)
- Origin (Prusak = from Prussia, Mazur = from Mazovia)
- An ancestor's given name (Jankowy = Jan's family)

**Genealogical Implications**:
1. Records may use the surname, the przydomek, or both interchangeably.
2. Different branches of the same family might use different przydomki.
3. Upon emigration, some families adopted the przydomek as their new surname (making "Prusak" or "Skrzypek" the official surname in America).
4. Searching requires checking both the formal surname and known przydomki.

**Example**:
A family named "Kowalski zwany Mucha" in Polish records might appear as:
- Kowalski (formal surname only)
- Mucha (przydomek only)
- Kowalski-Mucha (hyphenated)
- Kowalski vel Mucha (using Latin "vel")

### Suffix Patterns

**-ski / -ska** (masculine / feminine):
Originally indicated nobility or origin from a place. "Krakowski" means "from Kraków." Over time, commoners adopted the suffix, and it lost its noble connotation. The suffix changes by gender: Kowalski (male), Kowalska (female).

**-wicz / -owicz**:
Patronymic suffix meaning "son of." Adamowicz = son of Adam. Similar to Slavic surnames in Belarus, Ukraine, and Russia. More common in eastern Poland.

**-ek / -ak / -ik**:
Diminutive or descriptive suffixes. Nowak = "the new one" (newcomer). Kowalik = little smith.

**-ów / -owa / -ówna**:
Possessive suffixes. -ów (masculine possession), -owa (married woman's form of husband's surname), -ówna (unmarried daughter's form). Maria Kowalowa = Maria, wife of Kowalski. Anna Kowalówna = Anna, daughter of Kowalski.

### Name Changes Across Partitions

**Russification (Russian Partition, post-1863)**:
After the January Uprising, Russian authorities suppressed Polish language. Names were transcribed into Cyrillic with varying accuracy. Wojciech → Войцех (Voitsekh); Stanisław → Станислав (Stanislav); Władysław → Владислав (Vladislav).

**Germanization (Prussian Partition)**:
German scribes recorded Polish names in German orthography. Jan → Johann; Stanisław → Stanislaus; Wojciech → Adalbert (the Germanic equivalent). Surnames were also altered: Kowalski → Kowalsky or Kowalski; Wójcik → Woicik or Wojzik.

**Latin Church Records**:
Catholic records used Latin forms of given names. Jan → Joannes; Stanisław → Stanislaus; Maria → Maria; Anna → Anna. Understanding Latin naming is essential for pre-19th-century records.

## Partition-Era Records (1795–1918)

Poland was partitioned among three empires from 1795 to 1918. Each partition maintained different record-keeping systems.

| Partition | Territory | Languages | Record Types | Archives |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|--------------|----------|
| **Russian (Congress Poland)** | Central Poland: Warsaw, Łódź, Lublin, Kielce, Płock, Radom | Polish (1808–1868), Russian (1868–1918), Latin (church records) | Napoleonic civil registration (akta stanu cywilnego) from 1808; parish registers | Polish state archives (Archiwa Państwowe) |
| **Prussian** | Western Poland: Poznań, Pomerania, Silesia, parts of Mazovia | German, Latin (church records) | Standesamt civil registration (from 1874); church parish registers | Polish state archives; some records in German archives (for areas transferred after WWII) |
| **Austrian (Galicia)** | Southern Poland: Kraków, Tarnów, Rzeszów, Przemyśl, Lwów (now Ukraine) | Latin, German, Polish (from 1784 civil registration in some areas) | Church parish registers; civil registration varied by era | Polish state archives; Ukrainian archives (for eastern Galicia) |

### Russian Partition Details

**Napoleonic Code Civil Registration (1808–1868)**:
The Duchy of Warsaw (1807–1815) and Congress Poland (1815–1918) used a civil registration system modeled on the Napoleonic Code. Parish priests acted as civil registrars. Records were kept in duplicate: one copy stayed with the parish, one went to district courts.

Records from this era are in **Polish** and contain:
- Births: date, parents' names, father's occupation, godparents
- Marriages: ages, occupations, parents' names, witnesses
- Deaths: age, cause of death (sometimes), surviving spouse

**Russification (1868–1918)**:
After the January Uprising of 1863, Russian authorities mandated Russian language for civil records. The format remained similar but was written in Cyrillic.

### Prussian Partition Details

**Church Records (pre-1874)**:
Parish registers maintained by Catholic and Lutheran clergy. Catholic records typically in Latin with some German; Lutheran records in German.

**Civil Registration (1874–1918)**:
Prussia introduced secular civil registration in 1874. Standesamt (civil registry office) records are in German. They contain similar information to church records but under state authority.

### Austrian Partition Details

**Church Records**:
Parish registers in Latin, German, or Polish depending on era and local custom. Josephine reforms of the 1780s introduced civil registration requirements, but implementation varied.

**Cadastral Records**:
Austrian administration kept detailed land and tax records. Cadastral maps show property boundaries and owner names, useful for locating ancestral homes.

## Document Types

### Akta Stanu Cywilnego (Civil Registration Records)

Civil registration records created by state-appointed registrars (often parish priests in the Russian partition). These are distinct from church parish registers.

**Akt urodzenia (Birth Certificate)**:
Contains: date and place of birth, child's name, parents' names and ages, father's occupation, godparents' names, witnesses.

**Akt małżeństwa (Marriage Certificate)**:
Contains: date of marriage, names of bride and groom, ages, occupations, residences, parents' names (often with note if deceased), witnesses.

**Akt zgonu (Death Certificate)**:
Contains: date and place of death, name of deceased, age, occupation, surviving spouse's name, cause of death (varies), informant's name.

### Księgi Metrykalne (Parish Registers)

Church-maintained records of sacraments. Separate from civil registration in the Prussian and Austrian partitions; combined in the Russian partition (where priests served as civil registrars).

**Księga chrztów / Liber baptizatorum (Baptismal Register)**:
Records baptisms, which typically occurred within days of birth. Contains: date of baptism, child's name, parents' names, godparents.

**Księga ślubów / Liber matrimoniorum (Marriage Register)**:
Contains: date of marriage, bride and groom names, witnesses. May include banns announcements.

**Księga zgonów / Liber mortuorum (Burial Register)**:
Contains: date of burial, name of deceased, age, sometimes cause of death.

### Alegata (Marriage Supplements)

Alegata are supplementary documents attached to marriage records. They typically include:
- Birth certificates of bride and groom
- Death certificates of deceased parents
- Parental consent documents (if bride or groom was a minor)
- Banns announcements
- Military service exemption documents

Alegata are extremely valuable because they often contain birth records from other parishes, revealing geographic origins.

### Spisy Ludności / Rewizje Dusz (Population Registers)

Population counts conducted periodically by church or civil authorities.

**Rewizje dusz (Soul Revisions)**:
Census-like documents listing all members of a parish, often by household. Includes: head of household, spouse, children, servants, ages, occupations.

**Spisy ludności**:
Civil census records. More detailed censuses occurred in the Austrian partition.

**Księgi ludności stałej (Permanent Population Registers)**:
Russian partition records tracking permanent residents of a community. Useful for establishing family relationships and residency.

## Search Strategy

### Recommended Research Approach

**Step 1: Gather Family Information**
Collect everything known from family sources: names, approximate dates, villages, parish names, occupations. Even fragments help narrow searches.

**Step 2: Identify the Region**
Use Mapa Nazwisk to see where your surname concentrates today. Cross-reference with family knowledge of origins.

**Step 3: Search Geneteka**
Search your surname across all voivodeships ("Wszystkie"). Note which parishes and years show matches. Look for clusters in a single parish, which suggests the family's home village.

**Step 4: Cross-Reference with Regional Databases**
Depending on region:
- Wielkopolska: Also search BaSIA and Poznań Project
- Pomerania: Search PTG databases
- Lublin region: Check Lubgens
- Galicia: Check Gesher Galicia

**Step 5: Locate Original Documents**
Using references from indexes, find original documents in Szukaj w Archiwach or Metryki.genealodzy.pl. Note the archive, fond, and unit numbers.

**Step 6: Extract and Verify**
Read original documents to verify indexed data and extract additional details (witnesses, addresses, occupations).

**Step 7: Build Backward**
Use marriage records to find parents' names. Locate parents' birth records, then their marriage records, and continue building backward.

**Step 8: Check Alegata**
Marriage supplements often contain birth certificates from other parishes. These reveal where spouses originally came from.

**Step 9: Document Negative Results**
Record which parishes and years you searched, even if you found nothing. This prevents duplicate work and reveals gaps.

## Languages in Records

| Language | Where Found | Era | Notes |
|----------|-------------|-----|-------|
| **Latin** | Church records (all partitions) | Pre-1800s, continuing into 1800s for some parishes | Standard language for Catholic sacramental records. Formulaic; once you learn the template, most records follow it. |
| **Polish** | Russian partition civil registration | 1808–1868 | Napoleonic-era records. Clear, detailed, written in Polish. |
| **Russian** | Russian partition civil registration | 1868–1918 | Same format as Polish-era records but in Cyrillic. |
| **German** | Prussian partition (all records) | 1795–1918 | Both church and civil records. Includes Evangelical/Lutheran records. |
| **German (Kurrent script)** | Prussian partition | 1800s | Old German handwriting style, difficult to read without training. |
| **Yiddish / Hebrew** | Jewish records | All eras | Some Jewish community records; state records typically in partition language. |

### Reading Strategies

**Latin records**: Learn common formulaic phrases. Baptismal records follow predictable structures: "Ego infrascriptus baptizavi infantem..." (I, the undersigned, baptized the infant...).

**Russian records**: Cyrillic transcription of Polish names. Once you learn to transliterate Cyrillic, the content parallels Polish-era records.

**German records**: Kurrent script requires practice. Many resources exist for learning to read Kurrentschrift.

## Additional Resources

### Kresy (Eastern Borderlands)

If your family came from areas now in Belarus, Lithuania, or Ukraine, records may be split between Polish and foreign archives.

- AGAD (Archiwum Główne Akt Dawnych) in Warsaw holds many Kresy collections
- Belarusian archives for former Grodno, Brest regions
- Ukrainian archives for former Lwów, Stanisławów, Tarnopol regions
- Lithuanian archives for former Wilno region

### Archives Outside Poland

Some records were transferred to Germany after WWII or remain in German archives:
- Standesamt records from areas transferred from Germany to Poland after 1945
- Church records microfilmed before WWII

### Emigration Records

For ancestors who emigrated:
- Hamburg Passenger Lists (for emigrants via Hamburg)
- Bremen Passenger Lists (largely destroyed in WWII)
- US/Canada immigration and naturalization records (often list exact birthplace, especially post-1906)
- Ellis Island and Castle Garden databases
