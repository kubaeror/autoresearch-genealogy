# Source Hierarchy for Polish Genealogy

When genealogical sources disagree, use this hierarchy to determine which source is more likely correct.

## Why Source Hierarchy Matters in Polish Genealogy

Polish genealogical research presents unique challenges that make source evaluation critical:

**Multiple Record Systems**: Poland maintained parallel civil and church registration systems. Church records (księgi metrykalne) often predate civil registration and may contain different details than civil records for the same event.

**Partition-Era Complexity**: From 1795 to 1918, Poland was divided among Russia, Prussia, and Austria. Each partition introduced civil registration at different times, in different languages, with different administrative standards. A single family that moved across partition boundaries may appear in records written in Polish, Latin, German, and Russian.

**Record Survival**: Wars, fires, and border changes destroyed many archives. Understanding which sources survive and which are reconstructed or derived is essential for accurate research.

## Primary Sources (Highest Reliability)

Primary sources were created at or near the time of the event by an official authority. These form the foundation of Polish genealogical research.

### Civil Registration (Akta stanu cywilnego)

Formal vital records maintained by civil authorities.

**When introduced**:
- Congress Poland (Russian partition): 1808
- Prussian partition: October 1, 1874
- Austrian partition (Galicia): Late 19th century, varied by region

**Record types**:
- Akty urodzenia (birth records)
- Akty małżeństwa (marriage records)
- Akty zgonu (death records)

**Languages**: Varies by partition and era. Russian partition records may be in Polish (1808-1868), then Russian. Prussian records in German. Austrian records in German, Polish, or Latin depending on the period.

**Where to find**: Szukaj w Archiwach (szukajwarchiwach.gov.pl), local USC offices (Urząd Stanu Cywilnego), regional state archives.

**Why reliable**: Created by authorized officials at the time of the event. Signatures of witnesses, parents, and clergy provide verification. Standardized forms reduce ambiguity.

### Parish Registers (Księgi metrykalne)

Church records of baptisms, marriages, and burials maintained by Catholic and other parishes.

**Coverage**: Pre-civil registration (some parishes from the 16th century) and parallel records continuing after civil registration began.

**Record types**:
- Liber baptisatorum (baptism registers)
- Liber matrimoniorum (marriage registers)
- Liber mortuorum / defunctorum (burial registers)

**Languages**: Latin (most common in Catholic records), Polish, German.

**Why valuable**: Often more detailed than civil records, including godparents, witnesses, occupations, and sometimes house numbers. For periods before civil registration, these are the only vital records.

**Caveat**: Recording practices varied by priest. Some entries are brief; others include extensive family details.

### Alegata (Marriage Supplements)

Documents attached to marriage records to verify the identity and eligibility of the bride and groom.

**Contents typically include**:
- Copies of birth/baptism certificates for both parties
- Death certificates of previous spouses (if widowed)
- Consent documents from parents
- Banns announcements
- Military service documents

**Why exceptionally valuable**: Alegata often preserve copies of birth records that no longer exist in the original registers. When a birth register has been lost to fire or war, the alegata attached to a marriage record may be the only surviving documentation of the birth.

**Where to find**: Attached to marriage records in state archives, sometimes digitized separately.

### Rewizje dusz / Spisy ludności (Population Registers)

Census-like household surveys conducted primarily in the Russian partition.

**What they contain**:
- All household members listed together
- Ages, occupations, relationships
- Religious affiliation
- Sometimes property information

**Why reliable**: Compiled by local officials who knew the community. Provide household context that individual vital records lack.

**Limitation**: Coverage varies; not all areas or years are available.

## Secondary Sources (Moderate Reliability)

Secondary sources are derived from primary sources through transcription, indexing, or compilation. They provide access but introduce potential errors.

### Indexed Databases

**Geneteka** (geneteka.genealodzy.pl): The most comprehensive Polish genealogical index. Transcription errors are possible. Name standardization may obscure original spelling. Always verify against original images.

**BaSIA** (basia.famula.pl): Indexes for Greater Poland (Wielkopolska) region. Volunteer-created, quality varies by contributor.

**Poznan Project** (poznan-project.psnc.pl): Marriage indexes for Greater Poland. Excellent coverage but may contain transcription errors.

**Metryki.genealodzy.pl**: Scans and indexes for various regions.

**Why less reliable**: Transcribers may misread handwriting, especially in difficult scripts (German Kurrent, Russian cursive). Indexes may omit details present in the original.

**Rule**: Use indexes to locate records, then verify against original images.

### Death Records (Age Information)

Death records are primary sources for the fact of death but secondary sources for age and birthdate.

**Why ages are often inaccurate**:
- Informants (often neighbors or adult children) may not know exact ages
- Elderly deceased may not have known their own birth year
- Age rounding was common (ages ending in 0 or 5)

**Best practice**: Treat ages at death as approximate. When available, use the actual birth record to establish birth date.

### Cemetery Records and Headstones

**Limitations**:
- Dates may be from family memory, not documents
- Headstones often erected years after death
- Inscriptions may contain errors or round dates

**Value**: Useful for finding burial location, identifying family plots, and locating surnames. Treat dates as leads to verify, not as primary evidence.

## Tertiary Sources (Lowest Reliability)

Tertiary sources are compilations of secondary sources or records from memory. Use for leads, never as standalone evidence.

### User-Contributed Trees

**Platforms**: Geni, Ancestry, MyHeritage, WikiTree, FamilySearch Family Tree.

**Problems**:
- No source verification required to add data
- Errors propagate as users copy from each other
- Same incorrect data may appear in dozens of trees, creating false consensus
- Conflation of individuals with similar names

**Rule**: NEVER use user-contributed tree data without corroboration from primary or secondary sources.

### Oral History

**Value**: Points to family origins, migrations, and relationships worth investigating.

**Limitations**:
- Family stories often compress generations (a great-grandfather becomes a grandfather)
- Names and dates are frequently wrong
- Dramatic events may be embellished or invented
- Immigration stories particularly prone to errors

**Best practice**: Record oral history carefully, then treat every claim as an unverified hypothesis requiring documentary evidence.

### Published Genealogies

**Quality varies widely**:
- Some are meticulously documented; others contain unverified claims
- Always check citations; reject undocumented assertions
- Szlachta (nobility) genealogies are often more reliable due to armorial records and legal documentation requirements

|-------------|-------|-------|---------------|

| Civil death records | High (death date), Low (birth date) | High | Moderate |

## Resolution Rules

When sources conflict, apply these rules in order:

1. **Primary beats secondary beats tertiary.** A civil birth record outweighs a Geneteka transcription, which outweighs an Ancestry tree.

2. **Contemporary record beats later record.** A baptism register entry made at the time of birth outweighs an age stated in a death record decades later.

3. **Official record beats informal record.** A civil registration outweighs a cemetery headstone.

4. **Record made at time of event beats retrospective.** A marriage record created when the marriage occurred outweighs a family Bible entry made years later.

5. **Original image beats transcription.** When an index says one thing and the original image says another, trust the image.

## Polish-Specific Considerations

### Calendar Issues

**Julian calendar in Russian partition**: Until 1918, the Russian Empire used the Julian calendar, which was 12 days behind the Gregorian calendar in the 19th century and 13 days behind in the 20th century. Records may show Julian dates. Add 12 or 13 days to convert to Gregorian.

### Age Discrepancies

**Death record ages**: Ages stated in Polish death records are notoriously unreliable, especially for elderly decedents. Always seek the actual birth record.

### Prussian Records

**Standesamt reliability**: German civil registration (Standesamt) introduced in 1874 is considered highly reliable. Prussian bureaucratic standards were strict, and records are generally accurate.

### Church Record Interpretation

**Latin abbreviations**: Catholic church records use Latin abbreviations that require careful interpretation. Common examples:
- fil. leg. (filius/filia legitimus/a): legitimate son/daughter
- ob. (obiit): died
- sep. (sepultus): buried
- nob. (nobilis): noble

### Alegata as Last Resort

**Preserving lost records**: When original birth registers have been destroyed, alegata attached to marriage records may preserve the only copy of a birth certificate. Always check marriage alegata when searching for missing birth records.

### Name Variations

**Multilingual records**: The same person may appear as Jan in Polish records, Johann in German records, and Иван (Ivan) in Russian records. Surnames may be similarly adapted or transliterated.
