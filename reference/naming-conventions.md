# Polish Naming Conventions

A guide to Polish naming systems encountered in genealogy research.

## Introduction

Polish naming presents unique challenges for genealogists. Three factors create complexity:

1. **Historical partitions (1795 to 1918)**: Poland was divided among Russia, Prussia, and Austria, each imposing different administrative languages and naming conventions on Polish records.

2. **Class differences**: The szlachta (nobility) followed distinct naming patterns from peasants and townspeople, with different suffixes, predikaty (predicates), and herby (coats of arms).

3. **Hereditary nicknames**: The przydomek system created parallel identifiers that functioned alongside official surnames, sometimes replacing them entirely during emigration.

Understanding these systems is essential for tracing Polish ancestors across records written in Polish, Latin, Russian, and German.

## Przydomki (Hereditary Nicknames)

The przydomek (plural: przydomki) is the single most important Polish naming phenomenon for genealogists. Misunderstanding it leads to lost ancestors.

### What They Are

Przydomki are hereditary nicknames used to distinguish families sharing common surnames. In a village where five unrelated families were all named Kowalski, each family might carry a distinctive przydomek passed from generation to generation.

Example: "Jan Kowalski vulgo Prusak" means "Jan Kowalski called Prusak." The przydomek "Prusak" identifies which Kowalski family this Jan belongs to.

### Why They Existed

Poland has an extremely high concentration of common surnames. The ten most frequent surnames (Nowak, Kowalski, Wiśniewski, Wójcik, Kowalczyk, Kamiński, Lewandowski, Zieliński, Szymański, Woźniak) account for a significant portion of the population. In a single village, multiple unrelated families might share the same surname.

The przydomek solved this problem. While "Kowalski" identified dozens of families, "Kowalski zwany Prusak" identified exactly one lineage.

### How They Appear in Records

Parish priests and civil registrars recorded przydomki using various formulas:

**Latin terms** (common in church records):
- "vulgo" (commonly called): "Joannes Kowalski vulgo Prusak"
- "dictus" (called): "Joannes Kowalski dictus Prusak"
- "alias" (otherwise): "Joannes Kowalski alias Prusak"

**Polish terms**:
- "zwany" (called): "Jan Kowalski zwany Prusak"
- "vel" (or): "Jan Kowalski vel Prusak"

**Word order varies**:
- "Kowalski Prusak" (surname first)
- "Prusak Kowalski" (przydomek first)
- "Jan Prusak Kowalski" (przydomek between names)
- "Jan Kowalski vel Prusak" (with connecting word)

### Immigration Impact

During emigration to America, przydomki created surname confusion:

**Przydomek became American surname**: Because "Prusak" was distinctive while "Kowalski" was shared by thousands of immigrants, some families adopted the przydomek as their permanent American surname. A family known as "Kowalski zwany Prusak" in Poland might appear simply as "Prusak" in American records.

**Przydomek dropped entirely**: Other families dropped the przydomek and kept only the official surname, becoming one of many "Kowalski" families in America.

**Switching between names**: Some individuals used both names inconsistently. The same person might appear as "Kowalski" on a ship manifest, "Prusak" on a naturalization record, and "Kowalski" again on a death certificate.

### Research Implications

**Always search both names**: When you find an ancestor with a przydomek, search all records under both the surname and the przydomek separately.

**The przydomek may indicate origin**: Many przydomki have geographic or descriptive meaning:
- "Prusak" = the Prussian (possibly indicating migration from Prussian territory)
- "Mazur" = the Mazovian (from Mazowsze region)
- "Rusin" = the Ruthenian (from eastern borderlands)
- "Niemiec" = the German

**Siblings may use different names**: One brother emigrating in 1890 might become "Prusak" while another emigrating in 1905 became "Kowalski." They are still brothers.

## Surname Suffixes

Polish surnames carry suffixes indicating origin, patronage, or description.

### -ski / -ska (masculine / feminine)

The most recognizable Polish suffix.

**Original meaning**: Indicated connection to a place or estate. "Kowalski" originally meant "from Kowale" or "of the Kowalski estate." The suffix derives from the possessive/adjectival form.

**Noble associations**: The -ski suffix was historically associated with the szlachta (nobility), who took names from their estates. "Jan z Kowalskich" (Jan from Kowalski estate) became "Jan Kowalski."

**Later adoption**: By the 19th century, commoners widely adopted -ski surnames, often creating them from occupations or descriptions. Not all -ski surnames indicate nobility.

**Geographic examples**:
- Krakowski (from Kraków)
- Warszawski (from Warszawa)
- Poznański (from Poznań)
- Lubelski (from Lublin)

**Feminine form**: Women's surnames take -ska ending. Kowalski (male) becomes Kowalska (female).

### -wicz / -owicz

**Meaning**: Patronymic suffix meaning "son of."

**Examples**:
- Janowicz = son of Jan
- Piotrowicz = son of Piotr
- Adamowicz = son of Adam
- Pawłowicz = son of Paweł

**Regional distribution**: More common in eastern Poland and the Kresy (eastern borderlands including areas now in Lithuania, Belarus, and Ukraine). Shows influence of East Slavic naming patterns.

### -ek / -ak / -ik

**Meaning**: Diminutive or descriptive suffixes.

**Examples**:
- Nowak = the new one (newcomer to the village)
- Dudek = the hoopoe bird
- Kowalik = small smith, or son of a smith
- Bielak = the white/pale one
- Czerwiak = the red one

These suffixes often created surnames from nicknames or physical descriptions.

### -czyk / -czuk

**Meaning**: Patronymic suffixes, similar to -wicz but with different regional distribution.

**Regional notes**: The -czuk form is particularly common in areas with Ukrainian and Belarusian influence. The -czyk form appears throughout Poland but is also more frequent in eastern regions.

**Examples**:
- Pawelczyk = son of Paweł
- Adamczuk = son of Adam
- Marcinczyk = son of Marcin

## Szlachta (Nobility) Naming

The Polish nobility (szlachta) followed distinct naming conventions that require special attention.

### Herby (Coats of Arms)

The szlachta organized not just by surname but by herb (coat of arms, plural: herby). Understanding herby is crucial:

**Multiple families, one herb**: Many unrelated families shared the same coat of arms. The herb Jastrzębiec, for example, was used by over 600 different families with different surnames.

**Same surname, different herby**: Conversely, families with identical surnames might belong to different herby and have no relation to each other.

**Identification pattern**: Szlachta identified themselves with the formula:
- "[Given name] [Surname] herbu [Herb name]"
- Example: "Jan Kowalski herbu Ślepowron" (Jan Kowalski of the Ślepowron coat of arms)

### Predykaty (Predicates)

Nobility often used predykaty indicating estate ownership:

**"z [Place]"** or **"de [Place]"**: Indicates origin from or ownership of an estate.
- "Jan z Kowalskich" = Jan from the Kowalski estate
- "Piotr de Kraków" = Piotr of Kraków

These predykaty sometimes became fixed surnames, and sometimes appeared alongside them.

### Cautions for Researchers

**The -ski suffix alone does not prove nobility**: By the 19th century, commoners widely adopted -ski surnames. Never assume noble status from a surname alone.

**Verify with herbarz (armorial)**: Polish armorials catalog noble families and their herby. Key sources include:
- Kasper Niesiecki's "Herbarz Polski"
- Adam Boniecki's "Herbarz Polski"
- Seweryn Uruski's "Rodzina: Herbarz Szlachty Polskiej"

**19th century "ennoblement" claims**: Some commoner families adopted noble-sounding names or claimed nobility without documentation. Always verify.

## Name Changes Across Partitions

Between 1795 and 1918, Poland was partitioned among three empires. Each imposed different administrative languages on records.

### Russification (Russian Partition)

In the Russian partition (Congress Poland and the Kresy), records after 1868 were kept in Russian using Cyrillic script.

**Surname changes**:
- Polish endings adapted to Russian: Kowalski → Ковальский (Kovalsky)
- Some names translated: Kowal (smith) → Кузнецов (Kuznetsov, also meaning smith)

**First name changes**:
- Polish names given Russian equivalents
- Jan → Иван (Ivan)
- Piotr → Пётр (Pyotr)
- Stanisław → Станислав (Stanislav)
- Wojciech → Войцех or sometimes Альберт (Albert)
- Katarzyna → Екатерина (Yekaterina)

**Transliteration challenges**: Cyrillic has no direct equivalent for some Polish sounds. The letter "ł" might be rendered as "л" (l) or omitted. Diacritics disappear entirely.

### Germanization (Prussian Partition)

In the Prussian partition (Wielkopolska, Pomerania, Silesia), records were kept in German.

**Spelling changes**:
- Phonetic German spellings: Kowalski → Kowalsky
- "cz" sometimes became "tsch": Kowalczyk → Kowaltschyk
- "sz" might become "sch": Szymański → Schimanski

**Place name changes**:
- Poznań → Posen
- Gdańsk → Danzig
- Wrocław → Breslau

**Name translations**: Some occupational names were translated:
- Młynarz → Müller (both mean "miller")
- Kowal → Schmidt (both mean "smith")
- Krawiec → Schneider (both mean "tailor")

### Latin Forms (Austrian Partition and Church Records)

In the Austrian partition (Galicia) and in Catholic church records throughout Poland, Latin was commonly used.

**First names Latinized**:
- Jan → Joannes or Johannes
- Piotr → Petrus
- Paweł → Paulus
- Maria → Maria (unchanged)
- Katarzyna → Catharina
- Małgorzata → Margaritha
- Wojciech → Adalbertus

**Surnames**: Often retained Polish spelling even in Latin text, though diacritics might be omitted.

**Dates and formulas**: Latin abbreviations appear throughout: "die" (day), "mensis" (month), "anno Domini" (year of the Lord), "filius/filia legitimus/a" (legitimate son/daughter).

## Women's Names

Polish records handle women's names with grammatical endings and conventions that differ from English practice.

### Maiden Name Suffixes

Polish traditionally used suffixes to indicate a woman's marital status:

**-ówna / -anka**: Unmarried woman (daughter of...)
- Kowalska → Kowalskówna (Miss Kowalski, daughter of a Kowalski)
- Nowak → Nowakówna
- The -anka form used after surnames ending in -a: Poeta → Poetanka

**-owa / -ina**: Married woman (wife of...)
- Married to Kowalski → Kowalska or Kowalskowa
- Married to Nowak → Nowakowa
- The -ina form used after certain surname endings

### How Women Appear in Records

**Birth records**: The child receives the father's surname. The mother appears with her maiden name, often with "z domu" (née) or Latin "de domo."

**Marriage records**: The bride's maiden name appears, sometimes with both her father's surname and her own maiden-suffix form. The record shows the transition to the husband's surname.

**Death records**: A married woman appears under her husband's surname. Her maiden name may appear with "z domu," "née," or Latin "de domo."

**Example**: A woman born "Katarzyna Kowalskówna" (daughter of Kowalski), married to Jan Nowak, might appear as:
- Birth: "Catharina Kowalskówna" or "Catharina filia Kowalski"
- Marriage: "Catharina Kowalska" or "Kowalskówna"
- Death: "Catharina Nowak de domo Kowalska"

### Multiple Marriages

Women took each husband's surname. After a second marriage:
- Current husband's surname used in records
- Previous husband's surname rarely mentioned
- Maiden name may be omitted entirely in later records

This makes tracing women through multiple marriages particularly challenging.

## Regional Variations

Naming patterns varied significantly across Polish regions.

### Eastern Poland / Kresy

The eastern borderlands (now largely in Lithuania, Belarus, and Ukraine) show distinct patterns:

- More patronymic suffixes: -wicz, -owicz, -czuk
- Ukrainian and Belarusian linguistic influence
- Lithuanian naming patterns in the northeast
- Multiple linguistic traditions in single families
- Greek Catholic (Uniate) records alongside Roman Catholic

### Western Poland (Wielkopolska)

The Greater Poland region under Prussian control:

- Earlier adoption of fixed surnames
- More Germanic influence on spelling
- Better record survival in many areas
- Dual Polish/German versions of names common

### Southern Poland (Małopolska, Galicia)

The Lesser Poland region under Austrian control:

- Varied local naming patterns
- Austrian administrative influence
- Village-specific przydomki systems
- Mountain region (Podhale) has distinctive naming

## Search Strategy

When researching Polish ancestors, apply these principles:

**Search ALL name variants**: A single ancestor may appear as Jan, Joannes, Johann, Иван, Janek, and John across different records. Search each variant.

**Check both surname and przydomek**: If your ancestor had a przydomek, search records under both names. The family may have used either name in America.

**Consider partition-era spelling**: Determine which partition your ancestor's village was in, then search for German, Russian, or Austrian spellings of the name.

**Track women through maiden names**: Note maiden names whenever found. Search for siblings and parents using the maiden name.

**Watch for translation vs. transliteration**: A name might be translated (Kowal → Schmidt, both meaning "smith") or transliterated (Kowalski → Ковальский, same name in different script). Search for both possibilities.

**Document all variants found**: Maintain a list of every spelling variant you encounter for each ancestor. This becomes your search checklist for new record sets.
